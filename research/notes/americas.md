# Batch B — North & South America

Research date: 2026-09-22

## Overview

Eight rows: United States, Canada, Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Chile,
Colombia, Peru. The Americas are a genuine mix — Peru is (surprisingly) the
region's only country with a comprehensive binding AI law actually in
force, while the US and Canada, despite being AI powerhouses, both lack a
comprehensive federal AI statute and instead rely on patchworks of
state/provincial and sector-specific rules. Several South American
countries (Brazil, Chile, Colombia) have serious, detailed bills advancing
through their legislatures but not yet enacted.

## Things worth flagging for the fact-checker

- **Peru is the standout and most surprising finding of this batch.**
  Despite not being one of the "big" AI regulatory powers, Peru appears to
  be the first country in Latin America — and one of relatively few in the
  world — with a comprehensive, risk-based AI regulation actually in force
  (since Jan 22, 2026). I could not confirm specific penalty/fine amounts
  in the sources I found, which matters for the "enforcement_mechanism"
  claim — this needs a primary-source check against the actual Supreme
  Decree 115-2025-PCM text or an official government summary.

- **US federal preemption fight is live and unresolved.** The Dec 11, 2025
  executive order attempting to preempt state AI laws is being challenged;
  I did not find confirmation of any court ruling on its validity as of the
  research date. This is a fast-moving, high-salience story for US-based
  CFOs/councilmembers and the fact-checker should look for the most recent
  litigation status. I classified the US as "Sectoral or partial binding
  law" (reflecting the real, currently-enforceable state laws) rather than
  "Draft legislation pending" or "No known AI-specific governance" — this
  judgment call is worth double-checking, since a persuasive case could be
  made that federal preemption efforts make the "binding" status of state
  laws currently contested/uncertain.

- **Canada's AIDA is confirmed dead**, which contradicts a lot of older
  material (including likely training-data-era knowledge) that still
  describes AIDA as Canada's pending comprehensive AI law. Multiple 2026
  sources confirm it will not return and the government is pursuing privacy
  reform (Bill C-36) instead, plus a new $2B "AI for All" strategy that is
  an investment/policy package, not a binding law. Flag this for the
  fact-checker as a place where stale (pre-2025) information is especially
  likely to mislead.

- **Mexico**: found both a serious pending comprehensive bill (Feb 2026)
  and an already-enacted narrower law on AI use of performers' image/voice
  (April 2026) — treated these in one row since they're both accurately
  described in the fields, but the fact-checker may want to note the
  narrower reform as the more "real" binding governance today, with the
  comprehensive bill still speculative.

- **Argentina's provincial patchwork** (Buenos Aires, Jujuy, Río Negro, San
  Juan) is binding but I did not verify the specifics of what each
  province's rule actually requires — I described them collectively at a
  high level ("ethical use, human oversight, data protection in public and
  judicial sectors"). If the map needs province-level granularity this
  would need deeper research; I treated Argentina as one national row per
  the task's scope.

- **No access failures in this batch** — all searches returned readable
  content via WebSearch summaries; I did not attempt direct WebFetch on
  primary legal texts (e.g., actual bill PDFs) for every row given the
  volume, relying instead on law-firm trackers and regulations.ai/OECD.AI
  summaries cross-checked across 2-3 sources per country. This is worth
  flagging as a general limitation: none of these Batch B rows were
  verified against a primary official-gazette or congress.gov-equivalent
  text directly by me — the fact-checker should prioritize doing so,
  especially for Peru (enforcement details) and the US (preemption status).

## Confidence

All eight rows rated "medium" for the reasons above — directionally solid,
multi-source-corroborated, but not verified against primary legal texts.
